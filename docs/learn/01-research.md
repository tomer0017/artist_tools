# 01 — Research findings

Sources were checked on 2026-11-18. Each finding carries an evidence tag:

- **STRONG** — replicated or meta-analytic.
- **MODERATE** — some empirical support.
- **CONSENSUS** — broad expert or practitioner agreement, little direct
  testing.
- **PREFERENCE** — one school's method.
- **UNSUPPORTED** — do not claim.

Each reference carries a verification mark:

- **[V]** — the primary source was read.
- **[B]** — the bibliographic record was confirmed; the content comes from
  secondary sources.
- **[S]** — confirmed only through search snippets from the official site.
- **[NV]** — not verified.

## The short version

- **What the research supports most:**
  - drawing errors come mostly from how the subject is *perceived*
    (attention, visual memory, what people think things look like), not
    from hand control;
  - instruction and practice improve drawing.
- **What nothing supports:** any promise about how fast people improve,
  "right-brain" drawing, or upside-down copying as a way to draw more
  accurately.
- **Images:** CC0 and public-domain museum images are the safe core of a
  printable reference library. Unsplash, Pexels and Pixabay all have clauses
  that bear directly on a curated, printable library.
- **PDF:** jsPDF is maintained and the smallest; pdf-lib has had no release
  since 2021.

## 1. Observational drawing

- **Errors come mainly from misperceiving the subject.**
  - Cohen & Bennett tested the candidate sources of error. Motor
    coordination was a minimal source and misperceiving one's own drawing
    was not one. Misperceiving the object was the major cause [1].
  - Angle-perception biases predict the same biases in drawings [2]. Size
    constancy correlated with accuracy [3]; the shape-constancy finding [4]
    failed to replicate twice [5].
  - A 2016 review keeps perception central and adds decisions and schemas
    [6].
  - **MODERATE.**
- **"Learn to see like a camera" is not supported; "learn where and how to
  look" is.**
  - Artists are as affected by context as novices [7].
  - Artists' advantages fit better top-down attention and analytic
    strategies [8][9].
  - Skilled drawers encode structure faster [10] and remember more while
    copying [11].
  - **MODERATE.**
- **How people look predicts accuracy.**
  - Glancing more often between subject and drawing went with accuracy, and
    changing the glance rate changed accuracy [12].
  - Experts break complex originals into short, simple lines; beginners do
    not, and make errors of scale and proportion [13], though they draw
    single lines like experts [14].
  - Artists' strategies (frequent checks, segmenting, grids, construction)
    compensate for limits of visual memory and peripheral vision [15].
  - **MODERATE** (small samples).
- **Known schema errors are predictable, and naming the rule helps only
  partly.**
  - People draw the eyes too high on the head [16].
  - Telling non-artists that the eyes are about halfway down reduced, but did
    not remove, the error (randomised) [17].
  - **MODERATE.**
- **Individual differences are large.**
  - Weaker drawers are worse at copying simple angles and proportions, and
    at visual memory [18].
  - Art students tend to start a copy from its overall shape [19].
  - **MODERATE.**
- **Training improves drawing, with small samples and variable effects.**
  - A five-month foundation course improved drawing and some perceptual
    tasks [20].
  - A three-month course improved figure drawing, with no significant
    perceptual change [21].
  - A 16-week course improved some specific skills [22].
  - A randomised trial in adults over 65 showed sizeable rubric gains but no
    significant change in angle accuracy, with high dropout [23].
  - **MODERATE** that drawing improves with instruction. **UNSUPPORTED:**
    any rate, timeframe, or general change in perception.

## 2. Learning science for a visual-motor skill

- **Deliberate practice matters, but explains a minority of differences in
  skill.**
  - It explains 26% of variance in games, 21% in music and 18% in sports
    [25].
  - A replication of the original violin study found a much smaller effect
    [26].
  - There is no drawing-specific study.
  - **STRONG.** "10,000 hours" and "anyone can reach expert level" are
    **UNSUPPORTED** [24]–[26].
- **Interleaving helps learning to tell visual categories apart, including
  painting styles.** It works through contrast between styles [27][28];
  meta-analysis g = 0.67 for paintings [29]. **STRONG** for telling things
  apart; untested for drawing accuracy.
- **Spacing and mixing practice.**
  - Spacing is robust for verbal recall [30].
  - In motor skills, massed practice depresses learning [31].
  - Mixing practice types transfers in the lab, but only weakly in applied
    settings [32].
  - **STRONG** for verbal recall; **MODERATE** for motor skills.
- **Feedback helps on average but often backfires.**
  - It does worse the more it points at the person rather than the task
    [33].
  - Claims that a feedback schedule or attention cue speeds learning do not
    survive correction for publication bias [34][35].
  - **STRONG** that feedback about the task is safer than feedback about the
    person.
- **Left brain, right brain.** No whole-brain "left-" or "right-brained"
  types exist [36][37]. Drawing ability involves both hemispheres [38].
  **UNSUPPORTED** as an explanation of drawing [39].
- **Edwards' exercises, judged apart from her theory.**
  - Upside-down copying did not improve accuracy [40]–[42], although one
    study's judges preferred faces drawn inverted [43]. At most an optional
    warm-up.
  - Her five skills (edges, spaces, relationships, light and shadow, gestalt)
    map onto §1. Contour, negative-space and sighting exercises are untested.
    **CONSENSUS.**

**Proportion "rules" are averages, not facts:**

- **Eye line at half the head:** a useful adult average [17]. But the
  classical facial canons rarely held in 153 adults [59].
- **Figure height:** about 7.5 heads is the average and 8 heads the idealised
  figure [60]. Children grow from about 5.6 heads at age 2 to 7.5 at 15 [61].
- As fixed rules, **UNSUPPORTED**; as heuristics checked against the real
  subject, **CONSENSUS**.

## 4. Introductory painting workflow (practitioner consensus)

- **Value before colour; big shapes first, detail last.** Gurney plans in two
  or three values, then a big-brush block-in with limited colour [62].
  Speed's mass drawing [50]. Art students start copies from the overall
  shape [19]; that shows experts work this way, not that it causes better
  results. **CONSENSUS.**
- **Limited palettes:** widespread; no outcome studies found.
  **PREFERENCE.**
- **Canvas preparation:**
  - oil can go over acrylic gesso, with at least three coats against oil
    absorption [63];
  - "fat over lean" means flexible over less flexible [64];
  - **CONSENSUS** (manufacturers' guidance).
- **Transfer:** grid or squaring [57][58], or tracing and transfer paper.
  **CONSENSUS.** Transfer aids bypass freehand proportion skill rather than
  train it (an untested inference). Learn therefore teaches the grid as a
  transfer and checking tool beside measuring.

**For Studio Companion, safest first:**

1. CC0 or public-domain museum and natural-history images, with each item's
   rights flag stored with the asset.
2. Own or commissioned photographs and drawings, with a written licence and
   model or property releases.
3. Stock sites only after a legal review, and through their APIs as they
   require.

## 6. Print accuracy

- **Page size:** A4 is 210 × 297 mm (ISO 216), which is 595.28 × 841.89 pt
  in PDF; 1 mm = 2.8346 pt [76][77]. Sheet tolerance is ±2 mm, so the paper
  edge is not a ruler [76].
- **Scaling:** "Fit" shrinks pages to the printable area, to roughly 96–97%
  with common margins. Only "Actual size / 100%" keeps physical sizes
  [78].
- **The PrintScaling hint:** a PDF can carry `PrintScaling /None`, asking
  viewers to preselect no scaling [77]. Whether browsers and non-Adobe
  viewers honour it is not verified.
- **Calibration:** a printed test length, checked with a ruler, is standard
  practice in sewing patterns [82].
- **Margins:** printers leave 3–5 mm (or more) unprintable at the edges
  [79]–[81]. Recommendation: keep all content at least 12.7 mm from every
  edge, and keep measuring elements away from the top and bottom edges.
  **CONSENSUS**, derived from manufacturers' data.

## What this means for Learn

## What Learn must never claim

- Timeframes or speed ("draw in 30 days", "X% faster").
- "Right-brain drawing".
- That upside-down drawing produces accurate drawings.
- That drawing "rewires the brain" or changes perception in general.
- "10,000 hours".
- That a feedback schedule or an attention cue speeds learning.
- That proportion canons are anatomical facts.

## Unresolved questions

1. Should Learn ever measure accuracy (for example, angle or proportion error
   against the reference)? Proposal: no, at least in v1. Feedback can
   backfire, and the product's promise is less screen time.
2. Stock-photo sites: never, or only after a legal review (proposal: not for
   the library)?
3. Paper: A4 always, or Letter as an option for US users? Margin policy
   (proposal: 12.7 mm)? Rely on the PrintScaling hint as well as the scale
   bar (proposal: yes, both)?
4. Does the iPad's print sheet scale PDFs? This needs a test on the owner's
   devices before claiming physical accuracy from a tablet.
5. How much Hebrew text appears inside PDFs: labels and titles only, or
   instructions too? This decides font embedding and right-to-left handling.
6. How will Learn be evaluated before any outcome claim (for example,
   before-and-after drawings by volunteers)?
